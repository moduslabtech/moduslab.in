Why Apple Can't Make Enough iPhone Duos

Apple's first foldable sold out within minutes, and the part that keeps coming up is the hinge. We draw it, explain why a folding screen needs glass thinner than a hair, and do the maths a production engineer does: tolerance stack-up, and how small losses compound.

In short

Sold out in minutes. Pre-orders opened on 16 October at $1,999, and US delivery estimates soon slipped by months. In India it starts at ₹2,99,900. Apple hasn't said how many it built.

The hinge is the hard part. Apple says the hinge is engineered from more than 100 components. It has to swing through 180 degrees, hold the centre of the screen flat, and close with a magnet array, in a phone 5.2 mm thick when open.

Glass thinner than a hair. Bending stress grows with thickness and shrinks with the bend radius, so ordinary cover glass would shatter. Folding screens use ultra-thin glass about 30 microns thick, in a stack whose layers glide past one another like the pages of a book.

Small losses compound. If a hinge needs 100 parts and steps to be right, 99.9% each gives about 90% good hinges, 99.5% gives about 61%, and 99% only 37%. One Chinese report put final-assembly yield at about 60%; it's unconfirmed, and the video says so.

0:00 · $2,000 and sold out

Two thousand dollars, and you still can't buy one.

On the sixteenth of October, Apple opened pre-orders for its first foldable phone, the iPhone Duo. Within minutes, launch-day stock was gone. Half an hour later, Apple's own US store was quoting the twenty-fifth of January for every colour and every size.

On eBay, a pre-order for the two-terabyte model, which costs three thousand, one hundred and ninety-nine dollars, was marked sold at five thousand.

In India, the Duo starts at two lakh, ninety-nine thousand, nine hundred rupees. The two-terabyte model is four lakh, forty-nine thousand, nine hundred.

So why can't the richest phone company on Earth make enough of them?

Apple hasn't said. But it has told us what's inside. And there's one part that keeps coming up. The hinge.

So let's do what engineers do. Let's take it apart.

1:08 · The 100-part hinge

Apple says the Duo's hinge is engineered from more than one hundred components.

Compare that with the flip phones of twenty years ago. Their hinge was often just a pin, a spring and a barrel. A handful of parts. If it wobbled a bit, nobody cared, because the screen didn't bend.

The Duo's hinge has a much harder job. Balloon one: it has to swing two halves of a phone through a hundred and eighty degrees, every time, thousands of times a year. Balloon two: when it's open, it has to hold the middle of a seven point six inch screen perfectly flat. Apple says the hinge supports the centre of the display. Balloon three: when it's closed, it has to bring the two halves together, with a row of magnets, so they meet cleanly.

And it has to do all of that inside a phone that's five point two millimetres thick when open. That's about three coins stacked together.

2:09 · Glass you can roll like paper

Now look at what it's carrying.

The inner screen is a stack of thin layers. Apple describes a protective polymer on top, high-strength glass above and below the flexible display panel, and a titanium plate at the bottom.

Here's the problem with glass. Bending stress in a sheet goes up with its thickness, and down with the radius of the bend.

Apple hasn't published the bend radius. So let's take two millimetres as an example. Bend the glass on a normal phone, about half a millimetre thick, that tightly, and the stress would be close to nine thousand megapascals. No glass survives that.

Now make the glass thirty micrometres thick. That's what Samsung Display used in its first folding glass, thinner than a human hair. The stress drops to about five hundred megapascals. Still high. But chemically strengthened glass can live with it.

That's why folding phones use glass you can roll like paper.

3:15 · Pages of a book, and the crease

Apple adds one more trick. It says custom adhesives let the layers glide past one another, like the pages of a book. Bend a closed book and the pages slide. Glue them together and the spine cracks. Letting each layer slide means each one only feels the stress of its own thickness.

But this only works if the hinge puts the screen in exactly the right place. A fraction of a millimetre off, and the crease gets deeper, the screen ripples, or the halves don't close flat. Early reviewers were split. Some could barely see the crease. Others could see it at certain angles.

3:56 · Tolerance stack-up

And that brings us to the real reason this is hard to make. Tolerance.

No part is made to its exact size. Every part has a small allowed error. Say a hinge has ten parts in a chain, and each one is allowed to be off by one hundredth of a millimetre. In the worst case, all ten errors line up in the same direction. Now the total error is a tenth of a millimetre.

That's called tolerance stack-up. In the real world, the errors usually partly cancel out, and the typical total is closer to three hundredths. But some phones will land near the worst case. And on a folding screen, a tenth of a millimetre is a visible crease or a gap between the halves.

4:44 · The yield maths

So engineers have two choices. Make every part more precise, which is slower and more expensive. Or measure every finished hinge and throw away the ones that don't fit.

Either way, you lose output. And here's where the maths gets brutal.

Imagine each of the hundred parts, or each step in putting them together, comes out right ninety-nine point nine percent of the time. That sounds excellent. But the whole hinge only works if every one of them is right. So you multiply. Ninety-nine point nine percent, a hundred times over, gives you about ninety percent good hinges.

Drop each part to ninety-nine point five percent, and you're down to about sixty percent.

At ninety-nine percent, it's thirty-seven percent. Nearly two in three hinges fail.

This is a simplified model. Real factories check parts before they go in, and some faults can be reworked. But it shows why a part with a hundred pieces is so unforgiving. Tiny losses at each step compound.

5:56 · The honest part: the 60% report

Now, the honest part.

Here's what we know, and what we don't.

In late September, China's Jiemian News reported that only slightly more than sixty percent of assembled Duos were passing Apple's checks. That figure comes from one anonymous source near Foxconn. Apple hasn't confirmed it. And it's the yield of the whole finished phone, not just the hinge.

The same report said the hinge parts and the folding screens had both had low yields, late deliveries and design changes. And it said that, under Apple's current standards, the line might need six months to a year to mature. Suppliers were reportedly waiting to hear whether Apple would relax those standards.

6:43 · Rumours and delivery dates

One tech site went further. Citing Chinese media, it said Apple Store staff blamed the hinge for the short supply. We couldn't trace that to a named source, so treat it as a rumour.

The delivery dates also depend on who you ask. AppleInsider first saw orders slip to late November. Apple's own store showed late January soon after. Carriers have quoted earlier dates.

And Apple has never said how many Duos it built. Most launches, it doesn't.

7:17 · Samsung took seven generations

What is on the record: the research firm Counterpoint forecasts up to six million foldable iPhones shipped this year. That would already make Apple the number two foldable maker, behind Samsung. For comparison, the Jiemian report says a single iPhone Pro model usually ships twenty to thirty million in a full cycle.

And Samsung shows how long this takes. It has been making folding phones since twenty nineteen. Last year, Samsung Display said its latest folding panel survived five hundred thousand folds in an independent test. That's more than ten years of normal use. It took seven generations to get there.

Apple is on generation one.

8:04 · Marketing, or a ramp?

So is the shortage marketing? Probably not. A two-thousand-dollar phone that leaves money on the table for three months isn't a strategy. It looks more like a factory still learning to build a part with a hundred pieces, where every piece has to be right.

The good news is that yield problems are usually temporary. The line gets tuned. The parts get more consistent. The losses shrink. That's what ramping up means.

But for now, the most expensive iPhone ever made is limited by its smallest parts.

So here's our question for you. Would you pay three lakh rupees for a first-generation hinge? Or would you wait for version two?
